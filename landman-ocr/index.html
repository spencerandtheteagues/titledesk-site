Landman OCR

OCR that runs on the laptop, not in someone's cloud.

County recorders hand back scanned instruments, often as one multi-page image per document. TitleDesk reads them locally with Tesseract, page by page, and tells you which pages it could not read instead of recording them as blank.

Why on-machine OCR

  • Client documents do not leave the building. OCR text and extractions are produced locally; nothing is uploaded to an OCR service.
  • No per-page bill. The expensive part of a title packet — reading — costs nothing because it happens on your hardware.
  • Works offline. A day in a courthouse basement with no signal does not stop the reading.

Multi-page instruments

Recorders often return a whole instrument as a single multi-page TIFF. TitleDesk processes each page — including exhibits and lease schedules on later pages — and surfaces extraction failures for review. A truncated read of a multi-page instrument is exactly the kind of silent error that produces an empty report; it is treated as a defect, not as missing data.

Nothing disappears quietly

A page OCR cannot read is marked "needs your eyes". Thin OCR stays empty rather than inventing a plausible lessor. Each extracted date, party and fraction stays proposed until a person accepts it.

Classification after reading

Twenty-three instrument types — lease, deed, deed of trust, release, assignment, ratification, probate, unit designation, plat and more — are classified and filed into the submission folder your company expects. Filename beats body text, so a ratification does not steal the instrument it recites.

Throughput

A pool of OCR workers keeps several documents in flight, and the interface reports document and page progress while the packet is processed. Throughput depends on scan quality and your hardware; measure it on your own packet.

On real assignments completed by working landmen, TitleDesk's automated run takes about twenty minutes for work that takes a landman three to five days; the landman then reviews and certifies.
