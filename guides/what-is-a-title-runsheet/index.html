What is a title runsheet?

A runsheet is the backbone of a title opinion: every recorded instrument that touches the tract, in order, with the parties, the citation and the interest. Here is what belongs on one, what a good one looks like, and how to check it.

Definition

A title runsheet (or "run sheet") is a chronological schedule of every instrument of record affecting a particular tract of land, from the sovereign grant or a chosen starting point to the present. Each entry identifies the instrument by its recording citation and states who conveyed what to whom.

What each row carries

  • Date — instrument date and filing date.
  • Grantor and grantee — as written on the instrument, not normalized away.
  • Citation — record series, volume and page, or instrument number. Deed Records 244/240 is not Official Public Records 244/240.
  • Instrument type — warranty deed, deed of trust, oil and gas lease, release, assignment, probate, affidavit, plat.
  • Interest — what fraction or estate was conveyed, reserved or encumbered.
  • Remarks — legal description excerpts, acreage, prior references.

Where the rows come from

From the county's index — searched by abstract or survey, and by the parties in the chain — and then from reading the instruments themselves. An index row tells you an instrument exists; only the instrument tells you what it did. A runsheet built from the index alone is a list of citations, not a title examination.

What a gap looks like

The grantee of one deed should appear as the grantor of the next conveyance of that interest. When they do not, there is a gap: an unrecorded conveyance, a probate that was never filed, an heirship not of record, or a name variation. A good runsheet shows the gap. A bad one hides it behind a row that looks plausible.

Checking a runsheet

  1. Every citation resolves to an instrument actually located on the index — same book, same numbers.
  2. Every row can be traced to the page it was read from.
  3. Grantee-to-grantor continuity holds, or the break is stated.
  4. Liens and leases have their releases matched, or are listed as unreleased.
  5. Interests conveyed out of a tract total no more than what was owned.
